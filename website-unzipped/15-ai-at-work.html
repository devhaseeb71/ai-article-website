AI at Work — Productivity, Jobs and the Productivity Paradox

The research on AI and work is in its awkward early phase: large measured gains on specific tasks, disappointing results at the level of whole organisations, and very good reasons why.

2024-04-22• Applications• 1,105 words• CC0 1.0 public domain

The most-cited finding in the AI and labour literature is also the least intuitive: in controlled studies, experienced professionals using an AI assistant often finished a task in roughly half the time, with output quality sometimes higher. And yet firm-level productivity statistics have barely moved.

Both are true. Understanding why is the whole problem.

What the controlled studies found

Several well-designed experiments have looked at concrete, bounded tasks — writing a short professional document, summarising a meeting, drafting a marketing plan, answering support questions, coding a defined function. The pattern is consistent:

  • Large gains on tasks inside a professional's existing competence. These people are the intended users of the technology, and the assistant gives them a fast, competent first draft.
  • Gains concentrated in the lower tail of quality. The worst performers improve most, and the best performers sometimes do not improve at all.
  • Bigger gains where the task was once tedious. Writing, boilerplate, summarising, formatting.
  • Smaller or negative gains outside the frontier. One widely cited finding is that consultants given an AI tool were faster and produced more output, but the output was judged less correct overall. Doing a bigger volume of plausible work is not the same as doing the job.

The pattern is consistent enough to be a useful prior: AI assistance compresses the skill gap. It helps most where skill is scarce and helps least where skill is high.

Why the aggregate does not move

Four explanations, and they are not mutually exclusive.

Task-level gains are not job-level gains. A job is a bundle of tasks, most of which are not text generation. Meetings, negotiation, physical presence, institutional memory, deciding what is worth doing — none of these are accelerated. A firm whose value is in coordination and judgment gets less from a writing assistant than a firm whose value is in producing documents.

Time saved is not time banked. Measured time savings are frequently spent on more work of the same kind, or on coordination, or on the verification the new output requires. If half of a task becomes free, the rational response is usually to do more tasks, not to hand the surplus to the finance department. This is the standard reason a measurable micro-efficiency does not become a macro gain.

The output has to be absorbed. A world with more drafts needs more readers. Review capacity, integration and coordination are the constraint, and none of them are accelerated by the same tools. This is the strongest version of the argument: AI makes production cheap faster than it makes judgement cheap, so the bottleneck moves rather than disappears.

Measurement is early. Firms adopting AI seriously are, at this writing, mostly a few years into it. Investment, process redesign, data cleanup and training all precede visible output. Aggregates may catch up slowly, and we should be careful not to conclude from a short window that there is nothing there.

What happens to jobs

The evidence supports a consistent picture: tasks change faster than job titles do. Most occupations are bundles, and AI takes some parts and leaves others.

  • More affected: first drafts, routine correspondence, summarising, basic research, translation, standard customer communication, some forms of coding, simple data analysis, triage.
  • Less affected: work requiring physical presence, legal and fiduciary responsibility, negotiation with counter-parties, leadership under uncertainty, anything where a person must be accountable.
  • Changed rather than eliminated: most professional roles, where the drafting disappears and the reviewing, deciding and owning expands.

Two structural pressures are worth naming.

Entry-level work is the pressure point. The junior tasks — the summaries, the first drafts, the routine queries — are precisely the tasks being automated. Those tasks are also how a career is built: that is where people learn the organisation, the judgement and the context. Squeezing the bottom of the ladder is efficient in the short run and expensive over a decade, because it produces a shortage of people who can do the review.

The value of a task is not its difficulty but its complementarity. If a task is easy to automate and hard to verify, it becomes dangerous. If it is hard to automate, it stays valuable regardless of how routine it feels. A large part of career strategy now consists of moving toward the second category, and the most reliable version of that move is becoming the person who verifies the output.

The productivity paradox, resolved

The apparent contradiction dissolves once you separate three things that are routinely conflated:

LevelWhat improvesWhat it does not tell you
TaskTime and quality on a bounded, well-specified taskWhether the task was worth doing
ProcessFewer steps, faster drafts, shorter cyclesWhether the outcome is more correct
OutcomeRevenue, quality, safety, satisfactionWhether the process change was good

Almost all the measured gains are at the first level. Real business value lives at the third. The gap between them is organisational, and it is filled by management decisions — which workflows to change, who verifies, what to stop doing — not by better models.

What to actually do

  • Measure outcomes, not activity. A 40% drop in time-to-draft is not a result. A 40% drop in time-to-decision, with no change in error rate, is.
  • Change the process, not just the tool. Adding a generator to a process designed for slow careful work produces faster bad work. The work has to be redesigned around the new cost structure.
  • Budget explicitly for verification. If generation is 10× cheaper, review is now the largest line item. Design for it instead of pretending it is free.
  • Invest in intake quality. Poor inputs produce more output at lower value. Fixing the messy upstream process is often the highest-return AI project in an organisation.
  • Watch the entry level. If juniors do not get the drafting work, create the equivalent: structured practice with real feedback, on real code or cases, with someone accountable.
  • Decide what will not be automated in areas where the answer matters — hiring, credit, discipline, care. Declaring a boundary is a management act, and it is one only an organisation can make.

The broader point

The productive framing is not "will AI take my job". It is that the unit of work is changing, and almost every organisation still measures itself in units designed for the previous one. Teams that re-measure honestly, redesign workflows deliberately, and protect the training pipeline tend to do well. Teams that buy a tool, add a prompt, and hope will be disappointed — not because the technology failed, but because the change was never made.
