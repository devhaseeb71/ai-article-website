AI and Labour — Tasks, Wages and the Distribution of Gains

What the economics literature actually says about jobs and wages, why the scenarios are so contested, and which policy responses are supported by the evidence.

2024-06-24• Society• 1,348 words• CC0 1.0 public domain

Labour-market analysis of AI is unusually polarised between confident forecasts, each reaching different conclusions from similar data. The reason is that the question is being asked at the wrong level.

The evidence is much more coherent when you ask about tasks rather than jobs, and when you distinguish exposure from substitution.

The three questions, separated

Does the technology perform some of your tasks? For most knowledge workers, clearly yes, and increasingly at a level that was not foreseeable three years ago.

Does it replace your job? A job is a bundle of tasks plus a set of responsibilities, a workplace and a legal relationship. Replacing a bundle is a different project from performing some of its parts.

Does it reduce demand for your labour? This is the one that actually determines wages, and it is a demand-side question, not a capability question.

Most public argument confuses the first with the second and the second with the third. The evidence supports a clear answer to the first, a mixed answer to the second, and no clear answer to the third yet.

What the data shows

Task exposure is high and unevenly distributed. The tasks most exposed are the ones where language and pattern prediction are the core of the work: writing, summarising, routine analysis, first drafts, standard correspondence, some coding, customer communication. The tasks least exposed are those requiring physical presence, legal or fiduciary responsibility, negotiation, and judgment under uncertainty.

Exposure is not the same as automation. A task can be 80% performed by a model and remain a large part of employment, because the remaining 20% is what the employer actually hires for.

Aggregate effects so far are modest. The workforce statistics at this writing show real effects at the level of particular firms and occupations, and surprisingly small effects in aggregate. The interpretation most consistent with AI at Work is that measured task-level gains are being absorbed by coordination, verification and new demand.

The firm-level dispersion is the striking finding. Where adoption has been rapid, the same technology is associated with meaningful productivity gains at some organisations and none at others. The variable is not the technology. It is whether the organisation redesigned workflows, changed measurement, and upskilled people — which is a management variable, and it is the single most useful fact in this entire debate.

The early-career channel is the one to watch. The tasks most exposed to automation are disproportionately the junior tasks: the summaries, the first drafts, the routine tickets, the simple fixes. That is where people learn a profession. The concern is not that junior workers are displaced tomorrow. It is a slower erosion of the training pipeline that produces senior workers, which will not appear in statistics for several years.

Wage effects are being studied more slowly than they should be. The most plausible near-term distributional pattern is a widening gap between high-skill workers whose productivity rises and others whose effective wage does not. This is a distributional claim about this technology, and it does not require the general-purpose automation scenario to be true.

The scenarios, and how to read them

Gradual augmentation. AI improves a portion of many jobs, wages rise modestly, employment grows. This is the base case in most serious economic modelling, and it is broadly consistent with the historical record of general-purpose technologies: electricity, the spreadsheet, the container.

Task displacement in specific occupations. Within a decade, large shares of tasks in writing-heavy and routine analytical occupations are performed by machines, with headcount falling in specific entry-level roles. Reasonable, and already visible in some sectors.

Mass displacement. Large-scale unemployment from generalised intelligence. Serious analysts continue to produce these scenarios, and they cannot be dismissed — but they require extrapolations through several uncertain steps, and they are not the same class of claim as the previous two.

The honest framing: the further away the scenario, the less it should drive present decisions. Decisions about education, housing, insurance and social protection have to be made now, under uncertainty, and should be robust across the scenarios rather than optimised for one. That is a decision-theoretic argument, and it favours a specific response.

What the evidence supports as policy

Portable, individualised benefits. Benefits that follow a person rather than a job — health insurance, housing, education funding, basic income pilots — hedge across scenarios in a way that job-specific retraining does not. This is the most common finding in the serious economic literature, and it is not close.

Training with a verifiable outcome. Programs should be judged on employment and wage outcomes at 12 months, not on enrolment or completion. The evidence on reskilling programmes generally is poor, and the reason is that most of them teach tools rather than restructure the work people actually do.

Reducing the cost of hiring. Hiring friction — in some of the advanced economies, a significant part of why AI-exposed work is not being converted into displacement — is one of the most direct and least discussed levers available.

Investing in complementary capability. Human-AI collaboration quality varies enormously between organisations and is only partly a technology question. In medicine, law and engineering, the state of the deployment practice will matter more for employment outcomes over the next decade than the state of the model.

Keeping measurement honest. Labour statistics are lagging the technology by a decade. Better measurement of tasks rather than occupations, more frequent, disaggregated by demographic group, is a public good and is currently under-funded.

Antitrust. The most plausible route to worse labour outcomes is not the technology but its ownership — a small number of firms with the power to set the terms on which AI is deployed into workplaces. Concentration is a labour-policy question before it is a competition-policy question.

Making room for a different distribution. The realistic near-term distributional risk is not mass unemployment. It is a widening wage gap and a hollowing of the lower-middle wage structure, which damages social cohesion and demand, and is measurable now. Policies that address it — bargaining power, wage support, shorter working hours with the productivity gains shared — are the ones most likely to prove necessary regardless of which of the three scenarios occurs.

The part that is not economic

The most serious human cost of AI-driven work change is not unemployment. It is erosion of craft and professional identity.

The person who spent fifteen years developing judgement and now does mostly checking finds that the part of the job they valued has been removed and the part they find degrading has been left. This is well documented in medicine, law, journalism, translation and software, and it is not a labour-market statistic. It is a reason to be deliberate about which parts of a job get automated, not just which parts can be.

There is a related and more hopeful dynamic: for the first time, a person with a good judgement and no elite credentials can do competent professional work. Access has genuinely widened. Both of these are true at once, and a policy that only accounts for the second will produce a lot of people who got access to work they resent, which is a bad outcome for them and a weak one for everyone else.

The summary

The literature supports a high and uneven task exposure, modest aggregate effects so far, enormous variation driven by organisational practice, a real distributional risk concentrated in lower-paid work, and a serious question about the entry-level training pipeline.

The policy response most consistently supported by that evidence is to make benefits follow people rather than jobs, to judge retraining by outcomes rather than enrolment, to measure tasks more often, and to use antitrust to protect workers from concentration rather than from technology. The response least supported is betting the future on a single scenario, because the scenarios are not forecasts — they are possibilities, and the sane strategy is to be robust across them.
