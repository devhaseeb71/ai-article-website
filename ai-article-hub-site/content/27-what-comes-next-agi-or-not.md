This is the twenty-seventh article, and the only one whose title is a genuine question about the future rather than a description of the present.

It is also the article where I have to be most careful about what I do not know.

## First, what we can say

The capability record of the last decade is not ambiguous in one respect: **general-purpose AI systems that would have been unthinkable in 2015 are now ordinary tools.** Systems write working code, translate between languages, summarise documents, answer questions about images, call APIs and take multi-step actions. That happened. The disagreement is about what it portends, not about whether it happened.

Three further observations are robust and under-discussed.

**Capability improvements have come from scaling, and scaling has been continuous.** There is no evidence of a wall. There is also no evidence that the curve will continue indefinitely. Both statements are true and they are compatible.

**Evaluation lags capability.** A system can be excellent at something no benchmark measures, and this is currently the norm rather than the exception. Every forecast that relies on benchmark numbers is reasoning about a lagging indicator.

**Deployment has outpaced the evidence base.** The organisations deploying this in high-stakes domains are, for the most part, doing so with evaluation practices that would be considered inadequate in any other engineering discipline. Whether this is prudent depends on forecasts nobody can verify yet.

## The case that progress will continue substantially

**Sustained investment and compounding returns.** Hundreds of billions of dollars, concentrated effort, and no sign of a plateau. Money that size is not spent on a field believed to be near its ceiling.

**Algorithmic progress is not just compute.** Better architectures, better data, better inference-time reasoning, and better tooling around the model have all contributed. Each is independent of the compute argument, and each can continue.

**The empirical regularity.** Across many labs and modalities, measured error falls smoothly with scale along roughly predictable curves, and the ordering of what gets learned is reasonably consistent. If that regularity holds, more capable systems arrive without a discovery.

**Automation of the work itself.** AI research is unusually well positioned to accelerate AI research — generating candidate architectures, writing training code, running and interpreting experiments, and summarising the literature. Whether that loop compounds is the crux of the strongest argument, and it is genuinely unknown.

## The case for substantial limits

**The prior track record.** Predictions in this field have been wrong in both directions and often for the same reasons. Serious forecasters have been embarrassingly wrong on timelines, in a way that should inform anyone's confidence in current timelines.

**Physical and institutional limits.** Labs are power-constrained, chip supply is constrained, and the data supply for human-generated text is finite and already used. The bottleneck may move from algorithms to energy, from chips to capital, or from data to permission — none of which is trivially removable.

**The reliability gap.** Capability is not reliability. A system that performs a complex task occasionally, without knowing when it is out of its depth, is a different thing from a dependable one, and the gap has not closed.

**Missing ingredients.** We do not have a theory of what produces generalisation, persistent memory, grounded understanding or self-directed curiosity. Systems have fragments of all four. It is entirely possible that the last 10% is not a matter of scale, and the people best placed to know say the field cannot tell from the inside.

**Institutional constraints.** Regulation, litigation, chip export controls, public opposition and safety incidents could all slow deployment, and the historical record of technologies encountering institutional friction is not encouraging to those expecting frictionless progress.

## Why forecasting has failed, structurally

This is the part worth dwelling on, because it is the most transferable lesson in the whole collection.

**The target moves.** As the frontier advances, the question shifts from "will machines think" to "will systems pass doctoral-level exams" to "will agents do a year of work", and every reassessment reads as a previous overestimate. The definitions are retrofitted to the current state of the art, which makes it impossible to score a forecast.

**Expertise in the domain is not expertise in forecasting.** The people who most understand the technology are the least equipped to predict its trajectory, for exactly the reason that made them successful: they are experts at the present.

**Benchmarks are constructed by people who want to be wrong.** Test-makers design tests to be passed. A benchmark that measures current capability is a benchmark that measures current capability, not future capability. A widely used general-purpose benchmark is an achievement that is engineered to be temporary.

**Scenarios are published as forecasts and consumed as predictions.** The serious scenario work says, explicitly, that these are not probabilities. It is a losing sentence in public debate, and the debate proceeds as if they were.

**The discussion is dominated by the extremes**, for reasons that are structural: a person predicting gradual, uneven, politically constrained progress has no constituency, while a person predicting catastrophe has an audience.

> The honest position is a wide range of timelines, with no principled way to narrow it — and that is a reason to build good institutions now, not a reason to pick a date.

## Why the timeline is the wrong thing to argue about

The most consequential decisions being made right now do not depend on resolving any of this.

**Education curricula**, professional standards, and the licensing of professions are set over decades and are barely affected by which year general capability arrives.

**Regulatory frameworks** should be capability-triggered rather than date-triggered anyway. A regime that turns on thresholds of measured capability is robust to the forecast; a regime that turns on "after AGI" assumes the conclusion.

**Public infrastructure** — compute, energy, data, research funding, and the institutions that allocate them — is being built now and will exist for thirty years regardless of which scenario occurs.

**Labour market institutions** described in [AI and Labour ](24-ai-and-labour.html) are robust across scenarios because the risk is distributional, not extinctionary.

**The safety work worth doing** — evaluation, interpretability, oversight design, incident reporting, security — is worth doing under every scenario, and more of it is better under all of them.

The people making the worst decisions are those who believe the question is resolved, in either direction. The overconfident doomers produce demand for a control that does not work and erode the institutions that could provide one. The overconfident accelerators under-invest in the things that make capability survivable, and they do it on the basis of a forecast they have no more claim to than their critics.

## What I would actually watch

Rather than timelines, these are the indicators that carry real information:

- **Cost per unit of capability**, which has fallen by large factors and would be the key data point in either direction. Continued decline supports the acceleration case; a plateau is the strongest evidence for a wall.
- **Evidence of genuine long-horizon autonomous work** — systems doing a job that takes a competent professional weeks, reliably, with verification. This is a much better indicator than benchmark scores.
- **The ratio of supervised to automated AI research.** If AI is materially speeding up AI research, the timeline compresses; if not, it probably does not.
- **Compute and energy supply**, which are physical constraints with long lead times and are therefore an early warning system.
- **The rate of serious deployment in high-stakes domains**, which determines how much of the economy experiences these systems directly.
- **Whether evaluation catches up with capability.** A field that cannot measure what its systems can do is flying blind in a way that should be alarming to everyone.

## Where I land

I think the honest summary is: **substantial progress, continuing, unevenly distributed across capabilities and regions, with a real possibility of a much larger change within a decade and a real possibility of a long, slow grind of incremental improvement that changes society through cumulative effects rather than any single shock.**

Both of those are consistent with everything observed so far. Most forecasts fail because forecasters commit to one.

And the more I read the field, the more convinced I am that the second scenario is the one worth planning around, because it is the one that requires continuous good management rather than a single correct decision, and continuous good management is what a civilisation is actually capable of.

## Closing

This collection started by observing that nobody can define artificial intelligence, and that the field shipped anyway on the strength of measurement. That is the pattern to keep.

The capability questions are genuinely open, and the people best placed to answer them are the people whose expertise makes them least able to. The harm questions are not open at all: bias, hallucination, over-reliance, insecure deployment, degraded privacy and training-pipeline insecurity are documented, measured and mitigable today, with well-understood mitigations. [AI Safety and Alignment ](19-ai-safety-and-alignment.html) is where the research agenda is; [AI Governance and Public Policy ](26-ai-governance-and-public-policy.html) is where the decisions are.

A field that measures honestly, publishes failures, documents systems, keeps a human accountable at the points where consequences are irreversible, and treats capability gains as a reason for institutional care rather than for confidence — that field will be fine. So will the societies that build it.
