For most of the history of digital media, being able to produce meant being able to operate the tools. A song required instruments and a recording setup; a film required a camera, an edit, and money; an illustration required skill that took years. Generative tools removed that barrier almost entirely.

That is not a small change. It is the reason the argument is loud.

## What the tools actually do

The three modalities work differently and it is worth being specific.

**Image generation** is now a post-production tool more than a creation tool. Inpainting, outpainting, background replacement, restyling, upscaling, product mockups, removing an object, relighting a photo: these are edits to something that already exists, and professionals use them heavily and quietly. Full generation from a text prompt produces striking images and is less useful in professional workflows than people expected, partly because the results are hard to iterate on and partly because precise control still requires skill.

**Music and audio** generation works well for instrumental beds, sound design, jingles, scratch vocals and remixing. Full song production is constrained by the inability to precisely control structure, lyrics and section transitions — the same problem as long video. Musicians use it as a sketchbook.

**Video** has improved fastest and is still the most awkward. Duration, physical consistency and character consistency are the weak points; a person who changes appearance between shots, or a cup that moves, breaks the viewer's suspension immediately. Practical use is concentrated in short clips, pre-visualisation, effects plates, b-roll and localisation.

**Writing** is the most quietly transformed. Not because a model writes better than an author, but because the cost of a bad first draft fell to approximately zero, which changes the shape of writing more than its quality. ## The taste argument

The strongest argument against "AI has no creativity" is that most human creative work is heavily selection-driven: a writer generates many drafts and selects; a designer iterates; a photographer shoots a hundred frames. What humans consistently do is **evaluate and choose**, and that skill is not going away.

Where the model helps is in the generation stage, which it does very well and very fast. Where it does not help is the selection stage, because selection requires having a point of view about what should exist. A model can generate a hundred business names in two seconds; deciding which one to keep is the work.

The counter-argument is that the *interesting* part of the process is being automated out. If the pleasure of writing is reaching the sentence you did not plan, and the pleasure of photography is finding the frame, then automating generation removes the thing people were doing. Both arguments can be true, and which one applies depends on whether someone is producing for themselves or for an audience.

## Where the money is

The economics are already clearer than the aesthetics.

- **The cost of a plausible asset has fallen to near zero.** Posters, stock photography, background music, placeholder video, decorative elements, concept variations. This is the same shift as stock photography or digital printing: a large volume of work becomes commoditised, and the value moves to what is difficult to specify.
- **Value migrates to constraint and taste.** Directing, editing, choosing, curating, and knowing what an audience will accept.
- **Volume becomes free and useless.** If everyone can generate a hundred similar images, a hundred similar images communicate nothing. Distinctiveness is now the scarce input, which is a nice inversion of the previous scarcity.
- **Small production becomes viable.** One person can now produce a video, a soundtrack and copy for a product. The cost of a complete artefact drops for everyone, and the cost of a *memorable* one does not.

## Copyright and provenance

Legally, the picture is not settled anywhere and the answers diverge substantially between jurisdictions. The relevant questions are unsettled: whether training on copyrighted works is fair use, whether the output is substantially similar to a specific training work, whether authorship is a legal requirement for protection, and whether a generated work can be copyrighted at all.

Two practical points are worth separating from the legal uncertainty.

**Provocation is legally different from copying.** The claim most likely to succeed involves specific identifiable elements in a specific output — a recognisable character, a distinctive composition, a named artist's exact style in a commercial context. "Is this training fair use" is a genuinely open question. "Is this output a derivative of this specific work" is a much more concrete one.

**Provenance is becoming infrastructure.** Content credentials and cryptographic signing of the origin of media are being adopted by camera manufacturers, publishers and platforms. In a world where any image can be produced, the ability to verify where an image came from is worth more than the ability to generate one. The likely steady state is not "we can tell deepfakes apart" — that is a losing arms race — but "some sources are verifiable and some are not, and institutions prefer the verifiable ones."

## What is genuinely lost

It is worth being honest about the losses rather than only the gains.

- **Fluent execution as a route to mastery.** A generation tool can produce a competent piece of music without producing a musician. The path from not knowing to knowing runs through making bad things repeatedly, and a tool that short-circuits that path removes a route to understanding.
- **The economic floor under illustrators, stock photographers, voice actors, session musicians and junior designers.** This is real and it is happening now, and it was happening at a slower rate before generative tools.
- **Scarcity as a signal.** When a commissioned illustration means something, it is because it cost someone something. Reducing the cost of the artefact reduces the meaning of the commission.
- **Skills that were never valuable but produced careers.** Most skilled work is practice, and practice requires repetition that is in some cases now unnecessary.

## A workable creative practice

1. **Use generation for exploration, not for commitment.** Twenty options to choose from is a genuine creative gain; shipping the first output is not.
2. **Keep the human decisions visible.** If you cannot articulate why this version rather than another, you have outsourced the judgement and should expect the result to look like everyone else's.
3. **Be explicit about provenance** when it matters commercially or ethically. The cost of disclosure is falling and the cost of exposure is not.
4. **Learn the medium anyway.** The tool improves; the judgement does not come with it.
5. **Do not use models trained on the specific work of the artist you are imitating.** Beyond the legal exposure, it produces work that is worse than either of you.

## The bigger picture

Creativity was never about making artefacts. It was about choosing what is worth making, and communicating a point of view well enough that somebody else sees it. Generative tools make the making nearly free, which makes choosing the genuinely important part of the practice.

That is a deflationary reading, and it is the one the economics support. The optimistic reading is also defensible: when making is cheap, more people can make, more ideas get tested, and the bottleneck on good work shifts to taste and attention — which is where it was always going to shift.
